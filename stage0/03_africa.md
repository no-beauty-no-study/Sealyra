# Stage 0, Chapter 3: Africa

## 3.1  The Island That Drifted Away

Eighty-eight million years ago a fragment of what is now Africa broke loose from the parent continent and drifted out into the Indian Ocean. The fragment travelled across the water until it eventually settled in the position we now designate Madagascar, where it remained isolated from every other landmass on Earth for the next eight hundred thousand centuries. Within that long interval of solitude, the species that happened to inhabit Madagascar evolved freely along their own divergent trajectory, generating creatures that occur nowhere else on the planet. The most conspicuous of these are the lemurs, wide-eyed primates whose lineage had already diverged from the line that produced monkeys and apes long before any continent began to drift apart. The chameleon, which can rotate each eye independently and unfurl a sticky tongue twice the length of its body, comprises another Madagascar speciality. So does the baobab, an immense ancient tree whose swollen trunk can accumulate as much as a thousand litres of water in reserve against the harsh dry season, providing the dispersed villages of the interior with a living reservoir that has endured for fifteen hundred years per specimen. Madagascar is the closest thing the planet has to a laboratory of independent evolution: take a slice of Africa, isolate it for eighty-eight million years, and observe what manifests on the other side. What manifests is so distinct from the rest of African fauna that no other continent has ever produced anything quite like it.

**Q1:** What is the giant African tree, native to Madagascar, whose swollen trunk can store up to a thousand litres of water through the dry season?
**A1:** baobab

**Q2:** Which wide-eyed Madagascar primates belong to a lineage that diverged from the line that produced monkeys and apes long before the continents drifted apart?
**A2:** lemur

**Q3:** Which Madagascar reptile can rotate each eye independently and unfurl a sticky tongue twice the length of its body?
**A3:** chameleon

## 3.2  The Bones in the Ash

Fifteen million years ago Africa itself began to tear apart. A considerable fissure called the East African Rift opened in the crust and continues to expand to this day, splitting the continent slowly along a fault line that traverses several modern countries. As volcanic ash from each successive eruption settled across the rift valley, it buried whatever happened to be lying on the ground at the time, preserving the bones of long-dead creatures in mineralised layer upon mineralised layer of compressed sediment. In nineteen seventy-four, a team of palaeoanthropologists excavating one such ash bed in the Afar region of Ethiopia extracted the partial skeleton of a small female who had walked upright on two legs roughly three point two million years ago. They named her Lucy, and Lucy turned out to be one of the most remarkable fossils ever recovered from African soil. She was an australopithecine, an early hominid whose anatomy demonstrated that bipedal walking had developed in our ancestral line long before any significant expansion of the brain. Every modern human alive today, every Inuit hunter, every Tokyo banker, every Andean shepherd, can ultimately trace his or her descent back through this small woman in the ash. Lucy is, in a literal sense, the great-grandmother that even your own great-grandmother would not have recognised. She has been resting in a museum in Cleveland for nearly half a century now, but the volcanic dust of the Ethiopian rift remains where she truly belongs.

**Q1:** What is the geological fissure across eastern Africa, still slowly widening today, where the partial skeleton of the early hominid Lucy was excavated?
**A1:** East African Rift

**Q2:** What is the name given to the three-point-two-million-year-old australopithecine skeleton, extracted from volcanic ash in Ethiopia in 1974, whose anatomy demonstrated that bipedal walking developed long before brain expansion?
**A2:** Lucy

## 3.3  Out on the Savannah

Lucy's descendants, after roughly two million years of slow refinement, commenced the long emigration from Africa that eventually populated every habitable continent on Earth. Yet not every line of her descendants left. One particular branch chose to remain on the same African grassland where Lucy herself had walked, and that branch persists in the same place to this day. Human communities still living on the African savannah occupy the same food chain as their unspeaking neighbours, an intimacy with the wild that the rest of our species has largely abandoned. Lions, the heaviest cats on the planet, dominate the upper level of that food chain, hunting in coordinated prides whose members distribute the labour of pursuit and ambush. Giraffes, whose impossibly long necks developed across millions of years to crop leaves from acacia branches no other herbivore could reach, exhibit a calm aerial vigilance over the open plain and perceive distant predators several minutes before anyone else does. Zebras assemble in striped multitudes whose moving patterns confuse the visual processing of pursuing carnivores. Above the entire system circle the vultures, scavengers whose acidic stomachs can devour rotting carcasses that would poison any other vertebrate, and through that patient recycling they maintain the fertility of the soil itself. Stand on the savannah at dusk and you can feel the food chain operating around you in every direction. The system is older than any city, and considerably more honest about who eats whom.

**Q1:** What is the open African grassland on which human communities still share a single food chain with lions, giraffes, zebras, and vultures?
**A1:** savannah

**Q2:** Which long-necked African herbivore developed its anatomy across millions of years specifically to crop the leaves of acacia branches that no other animal could reach?
**A2:** giraffe

**Q3:** Which African scavengers circle the savannah food chain, possessing acidic stomachs capable of digesting rotting carcasses that would poison any other vertebrate?
**A3:** vulture

## 3.4  The Cousins We Left Behind

While one branch of African primates wandered out across the world, several other branches stayed in the equatorial rainforest of the Congo basin and developed lives entirely unlike our own. Three of these closely related apes now share that vast green canopy with one another, and the contrast between their three societies is the single most instructive comparison in the natural world. Chimpanzees, our nearest cousin by genetic measure, live in restless political troops in which an alpha male prevails through carefully maintained alliances and the occasional outburst of strategic violence. Bonobos, who differ from chimpanzees only slightly in anatomy, have devised an utterly different solution to the problem of conflict: in bonobo society, almost every potential dispute is mediated through sexual contact, and the dominant individuals are typically female. Gorillas, the largest of the three and the gentlest by reputation, live in quiet harems guided by a single silverback whose authority is rarely challenged by anyone. Three apes, each genetically almost identical to the others, and each one demonstrates a radically distinct answer to the same evolutionary question of how to organise a society. Spend a long afternoon in their forest and the obvious follow-up question is no longer how we differ from the apes, but how the apes have managed to differ so dramatically from each other.

**Q1:** Which of our closest African relatives resolves potential disputes through sexual contact rather than aggression, and lives in a society where females are usually dominant?
**A1:** bonobo

**Q2:** Which African ape, our nearest cousin by genetic measure, lives in restless political troops in which an alpha male prevails through carefully maintained alliances and strategic violence?
**A2:** chimpanzee

**Q3:** What term denotes the single dominant gorilla male whose authority guides a quiet harem within the Congo rainforest?
**A3:** silverback

## 3.5  When the Sahara was Green

The greatest desert on Earth has not always been a desert. Ten thousand years ago, the region we now call the Sahara was a sprawling network of lakes and grasslands fed by an Atlantic monsoon that swept reliably northward each summer, and the cliffs throughout the region still display vivid rock paintings made by the people who once lived there: paintings of cattle, of swimmers, of hippopotamus and water buffalo wading across rivers that have not flowed for thousands of years. Then, roughly three thousand years ago, the orbital tilt of the Earth altered by a fraction of a degree, the monsoon track shifted south, and the lakes evaporated one by one. As the vegetation deteriorated, the soil dried and dust accumulated, and the entire region transformed into the harshest landscape on the continent. Today the Sahara extends across an area larger than the continental United States, and yet beneath its dunes the bones of crocodiles and turtles are still being excavated from the dried beds of former lakes, the geological evidence of a wetter past that the desert has carefully entombed. The lesson here is uncomfortable but worth absorbing: climate, on the timescale of civilisations, is not the static backdrop human history likes to assume it is. Climate has moods, and when those moods change, entire ways of life are simply terminated.

**Q1:** What was the great northern African desert ten thousand years ago, when seasonal Atlantic winds sustained it as a fertile network of lakes, grasslands, hippopotamus, and water buffalo?
**A1:** Sahara

**Q2:** Which seasonal Atlantic wind, which formerly swept reliably northward each summer and sustained the Sahara as a green landscape, eventually shifted south and abandoned the region to drought?
**A2:** monsoon

**Q3:** What ancient visual records, still preserved on the cliffs throughout the modern Sahara, depict the cattle, swimmers, hippos, and buffalo of the once-fertile green Sahara?
**A3:** rock painting

## 3.6  Minds Other Than Our Own

The fauna south of the Sahara turn out to be considerably more cognitively sophisticated than the older textbooks ever anticipated. An African grey parrot named Alex, studied for three decades by a researcher in Boston, mastered a vocabulary of around three hundred English words and, more remarkably, was able to articulate genuine questions about colour, shape, and quantity that no previous animal had been observed to ask spontaneously. Elephants, when one of their number dies, gather around the body and stand in something resembling a vigil, returning to the bones repeatedly across the following years, occasionally caressing the skull with a trunk that has clearly retained memory. Chimpanzees in laboratory tasks have repeatedly refused unfair offers of food: when one chimp watches another receive a sweeter reward for the same work, the disadvantaged chimp will throw its own reward back at the experimenter in evident protest, exhibiting what looks unmistakably like indignation at injustice. None of these examples manifests human intelligence in disguise. They manifest minds that evolved along different paths from ours, capable of grief, of grievance, of arithmetic, of a stubborn insistence on fair treatment. We are not, in the end, the only species on the continent capable of being upset about the way things are going.

**Q1:** Which famous African parrot, studied for three decades in Boston, mastered a vocabulary of around three hundred English words and asked spontaneous questions about colour and shape?
**A1:** Alex (African grey parrot)

**Q2:** What ritual-like behaviour do elephants perform when one of their number dies, gathering around the body and returning to the bones repeatedly across the following years?
**A2:** vigil

**Q3:** Which human-like emotional response do laboratory chimpanzees appear to manifest when they observe another chimp receive a sweeter reward for the same work?
**A3:** indignation

## 3.7  Cities Built Without Hands

Africa contains a great number of cities that no human ever designed. Army ants, which inhabit the equatorial forests in colonies of up to twenty million individuals, possess no permanent address: each evening the entire colony links its bodies together to form a temporary bivouac, then dismantles itself at dawn and marches several hundred metres to a new location, devouring almost every insect, lizard, and small mammal in its path along the way. Termites, by contrast, are the most accomplished architects on the continent. The mounds they construct in the savannah can ascend several metres above the surrounding ground, and beneath each mound a network of intricate ventilation shafts coordinates a passive air-conditioning system that maintains a steady internal temperature even when the outside air fluctuates by more than thirty degrees across a single day. Modern human architects in Zimbabwe have copied the termite design directly to climate-control office buildings without using electric air-conditioning at all. The lesson once again is unflattering for our species: complex engineering, social coordination, and durable construction were available on this continent long before any primate climbed down from the trees. We did not invent the city. We merely scaled up something the ants and the termites had perfected millions of years before we arrived.

**Q1:** What are the tall earthen African structures, built by colonies of social insects, whose intricate ventilation shafts maintain a steady internal temperature in defiance of harsh outside fluctuations?
**A1:** termite mound

**Q2:** Which equatorial African insects inhabit colonies of up to twenty million individuals and possess no permanent address, marching to a new location each day and devouring almost every small creature in their path?
**A2:** army ants

**Q3:** What is the temporary nightly structure that army ants construct from their own interlinked bodies before dismantling it again at dawn?
**A3:** bivouac

## 3.8  Gold, Salt, and the Books of Timbuktu

On the southern edge of the Sahara, where the desert begins to yield to the savannah, a trading city flourished across the fourteenth century that today most outsiders cannot quite locate on a map. Its name was Timbuktu, and through its dusty markets passed three commodities of staggering economic significance: gold mined in the rainforests to the south, salt extracted from desert pans to the north, and manuscripts copied painstakingly by hand in classical Arabic. At its peak, Timbuktu accommodated something like one hundred thousand inhabitants and amassed a private and institutional library reputed to contain several hundred thousand books, an accumulation of scholarship that rivalled any European university of the period. Caravans of several thousand camels would traverse the desert in convoys, transporting bars of salt south and bars of gold north, while scholars at the city's three principal mosques compiled commentaries on law, astronomy, medicine, and theology that circulated throughout the Islamic world. The European traveller who finally reached Timbuktu in the early nineteenth century was disappointed to find it half-empty, the wells silted, the libraries dispersed. Yet the manuscripts had not vanished. They had simply been buried in private cellars and in the dry desert sand by descendants who would rather conceal their inheritance than surrender it. Some of those manuscripts are still being unearthed today.

**Q1:** Which medieval African city, on the southern edge of the Sahara, became a hub for the exchange of gold, salt, and several hundred thousand handwritten Islamic manuscripts?
**A1:** Timbuktu

**Q2:** What carefully hand-copied Arabic documents, numbering in the hundreds of thousands, formed the great scholarly inheritance of medieval Timbuktu, much of which was buried in private cellars rather than surrendered?
**A2:** manuscript

**Q3:** What convoy of several thousand camels would traverse the Sahara to transport bars of salt south and bars of gold north through medieval Timbuktu?
**A3:** caravan

## 3.9  The Plague Reaches Cairo

In the same century during which Timbuktu was at its scholarly peak, an invisible catastrophe was propagating across the trade routes that linked Africa to the rest of the known world. The catastrophe was the bubonic plague, a bacterial pathogen carried by fleas which themselves rode on the black rats that infested every grain shipment, every caravan, every harbour. The contagion appears to have originated in the steppes of Central Asia, and it transmitted itself patiently along the Silk Road, accelerating each time it encountered a densely populated city. When the plague reached Cairo, then the largest city in the Mediterranean basin, it killed an estimated third of the urban population within three months. Bodies accumulated faster than the cemeteries could absorb them. Trade ceased. Markets emptied. The mosques, which would normally have been crowded with worshippers, fell silent except for the recitation of funeral rites. The Egyptian sultan attempted to quarantine entire districts, but no medieval authority understood the mechanism of transmission, and the pathogen continued to circulate unimpeded among rats and humans alike. The plague taught medieval Africa, and through Africa the rest of the connected world, a single uncomfortable lesson that we have had to relearn at intervals ever since: a city is only as resilient as the smallest pathogen it cannot detect.

**Q1:** Which medieval pandemic, originating in the Asian steppes and transmitted along the Silk Road, eradicated roughly a third of medieval Cairo's population within three months?
**A1:** plague (bubonic plague)

**Q2:** Which small parasitic insect, riding on the back of the black rat, transmitted the bubonic plague along the Silk Road from Central Asia to medieval Cairo?
**A2:** flea

**Q3:** What public-health measure did the Egyptian sultan attempt to apply to entire districts of Cairo in the medieval effort to slow the spread of the plague?
**A3:** quarantine

## 3.10  The Boy Who Built the Wind

In two thousand and two a brutal famine devastated southern Malawi after the rains failed for the third consecutive year. Crops withered in the fields. Livestock perished. Families divided their last reserves of maize porridge between the youngest children and themselves. A fourteen-year-old boy named William Kamkwamba, whose own family could no longer afford his school fees, walked instead to the small village library, borrowed a battered American physics textbook, and observed an illustration of a windmill on page sixty-six of the volume. The illustration demonstrated that rotating blades could induce an electric current sufficient to illuminate a light bulb. William assembled the components from whatever he could scavenge: a bicycle dynamo, a tractor fan, plastic pipes from a sugarcane plantation, blue gum poles, and rusted nails extracted from abandoned wreckage. Six months later, against the open scepticism of his neighbours, a five-metre wooden tower ascended above the family compound, its propeller turning steadily in the dry wind, and four light bulbs illuminated the inside of his parents' house for the first time in their married lives. The same windmill eventually powered radios and irrigation pumps, and a single boy with a borrowed textbook, in a country the rest of the world had largely written off, had effectively manifested electricity in a village the national grid had never reached. There is a lesson here about what scarcity and a library together can do, and it is not a small one.

**Q1:** What machine, built from a bicycle dynamo, a tractor fan, blue gum poles, and rusted scrap, did William Kamkwamba construct to bring electricity to his Malawian village during the 2002 famine?
**A1:** windmill

**Q2:** Which bicycle component, repurposed by William Kamkwamba, converted the rotation of his wooden blades into the electric current that finally illuminated his parents' house?
**A2:** dynamo

**Q3:** What modest village resource did William Kamkwamba walk to when his family could no longer afford his school fees, and which provided the physics illustration that started the entire project?
**A3:** library
