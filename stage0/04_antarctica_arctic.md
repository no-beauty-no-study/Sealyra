# Stage 0, Chapter 4: Antarctica and the Arctic

---

## 4.1  The Continent That Froze

Antarctica once supported a temperate landscape of forests through which dinosaurs walked and early mammals foraged. Then the continent drifted slowly southward to its present position above the pole, and a vast new ocean current we now designate the circumpolar current began to circulate around it, sealing every warm tropical stream out of the region and locking the cold against itself. Within a few hundred thousand years the entire landmass was encompassed by a kilometre-thick ice sheet, and every forest, every dinosaur, every warmer ecosystem inside it was simply buried alive. Today Antarctica constitutes the coldest, driest, windiest continent on the planet, a vast white desert beneath which the preserved fossils of those vanished forests still wait in the frozen rock.

## 4.2  The Krill Bonanza

That same circumpolar current does more than isolate the continent. As the cold water circulates, it pumps deep nutrients upward toward the surface, and the sunlight reaching the upper sea provokes an explosion of phytoplankton, which in turn sustains a tiny pink crustacean called krill. Krill swarm in shoals so dense that they tint entire patches of the Southern Ocean red, and from that single shoaling species the rest of the Antarctic food chain ascends. Blue whales, the largest creatures the planet has ever generated, migrate annually to these waters and filter krill through baleen plates by the tonne. Penguins dive after them, seals devour them, humpbacks sing through them. Without the patient upwelling of nutrients that the circumpolar current induces, none of this concentrated abundance would persist for a single season.

## 4.3  The Long Winter Watch

The Antarctic ice imposes a peculiar parental rule on its most famous resident. Once a female emperor penguin has laid her single egg, she immediately transfers it onto her partner's feet and traverses several hundred kilometres back to open water to feed. The male incubates the egg on his feet beneath a brood pouch of warm skin for four consecutive months, standing motionless in temperatures that descend below minus sixty degrees, surviving on stored fat alone while the polar night annihilates every other source of warmth. Hundreds of males huddle together in a continuously rotating mass that distributes the cold equally and prevents any individual from freezing in the wind. When the chicks finally hatch, the females return from the sea with their stomachs full of fish, and exhausted fathers stagger back across the ice in their turn.

## 4.4  Smarter Predators

A simple food chain forces every predator on it to develop more elaborate strategies. Orcas have learned to coordinate in teams of three: one whale generates a wave that washes a seal off an ice floe, while the others wait below to intercept the seal as it falls into the water. Leopard seals lurk just beneath the surface near a crack in the ice and wait silently for penguins returning from a fishing dive, then accelerate upward and seize the bird in a single explosive lunge. Each predator has devised a specialised technique tuned exactly to its single available prey, and the result is the most intellectually demanding food chain on the planet, played out across an ice landscape in which a single mistake terminates whatever creature made it.

## 4.5  The Lower Limit of Life

The entire Antarctic continent supports only one species of true insect, a flightless midge two millimetres long that inhabits patches of moss along the warmer coastal margins. It withstands temperatures down to minus fifteen degrees by generating an antifreeze protein in its body fluids which prevents ice crystals from rupturing its cells. Below that threshold even this stubborn creature cannot persist, and beyond a thin band of moss and lichen the continent supports no plant life at all. The Antarctic midge therefore constitutes the lower limit of complex terrestrial life on the planet, a small dark speck demonstrating exactly how cold a creature can endure before the laws of biochemistry intervene.

## 4.6  The Race to the Pole

Two expeditions raced across the Antarctic ice to be the first to reach the South Pole. The Norwegian explorer Amundsen prepared meticulously: sled dogs trained from infancy, a route surveyed in advance, food caches buried at every measured interval. He reached the pole, planted his country's flag, and returned without losing a single member of his party. The British captain Scott, by contrast, insisted on dragging ponies and motor sledges across terrain that demolished both, and arrived at the pole a month after Amundsen to find the Norwegian flag already there. On the return journey Scott and his four companions perished one by one inside their tent during a blizzard, eleven miles short of a food cache that would have saved them. Preparation, the Antarctic concluded, is not optional in this climate.

## 4.7  Endurance Among the Floes

The British explorer Shackleton once attempted to traverse the entire Antarctic continent on foot. His ship Endurance was caught in pack ice before it ever reached the coast, and the ice gradually crushed the wooden hull until it splintered and sank, marooning twenty-eight men on a drifting floe in the middle of the Weddell Sea. For more than two years the party persisted on that ice, sleeping in salvaged tents, consuming first their ship's provisions and then their sled dogs. When the floe finally fragmented into pieces too small to inhabit, Shackleton led six men in a small lifeboat across one of the most violent stretches of ocean on Earth to a remote whaling station, then organised a rescue mission for the rest of his crew. Every single man came home alive.

## 4.8  The Treaty and the Air of the Past

Twelve nations signed a treaty designating the entire Antarctic continent as a scientific reserve, prohibiting all military activity and weapons testing and reserving the territory for collaborative research. Decades later, scientists drilling deep cores into the ice extracted bubbles of air that had been preserved within the ice for nearly a million years, allowing them to measure directly the atmospheric composition of every climate the planet had endured across that span. The same scientists discovered, through routine monitoring, that the ozone layer above the continent was thinning catastrophically because of chlorofluorocarbon chemicals released by spray cans and refrigerators all over the world. The discovery provoked the most successful environmental agreement ever signed, which eliminated those chemicals globally and is now slowly permitting the ozone layer to recover. Antarctica had given the planet a warning it could read because the cold there had preserved it.

## 4.9  The Sea That Is Not a Continent

The Arctic, in contrast to Antarctica, is not a continent at all but an ocean surrounded by land. Across its surface a vast layer of pack ice expands every winter and retreats every summer, and across that drifting ice the polar bear pursues its single principal prey. The bear waits motionlessly at a breathing hole in the ice until a seal surfaces, then strikes with a single explosive lunge. Narwhals, the deepwater whales whose males generate a single spiral tusk reminiscent of a unicorn's horn, swim beneath the ice and surface at shifting cracks to breathe. Walruses pry open breathing holes in the fresh ice with their own tusks. The Arctic tern, the bird with the longest annual migration on Earth, traverses to the Antarctic and back every year, accumulating a lifetime flight distance equivalent to several round trips to the Moon.

## 4.10  Snow Bricks and Solar Storms

The Inuit of the Arctic constructed shelters from compacted snow bricks cut and stacked into a domed structure called an igloo, whose internal temperature ascends more than fifty degrees above the temperature outside on the strength of nothing but a single body and an oil lamp. They hunted seal, walrus, whale, and polar bear with harpoons and dog sleds, and inhabited every coastline north of the tree line for thousands of years. Further east, in Lapland, the Sami people migrated alongside reindeer herds across the seasons, treating the entire taiga as a single shared territory between human and animal. Across the long polar nights, the sky above both peoples could suddenly ignite with the green and violet curtains of the aurora, which is what happens when charged particles streaming from a solar storm collide with the upper atmosphere.

## 4.11  The Freezer Becomes the Arsonist

The pack ice of the Arctic now shrinks measurably every summer, and polar bears who cannot find a stable floe must swim until they exhaust themselves and drown. The thaw is opening the long-frozen Northwest Passage to commercial shipping, and Russia, Canada, Denmark, and the United States are already manoeuvring to claim the new sea lanes and the petroleum deposits beneath them. A deeper crisis lurks within the permafrost itself. The frozen soil of Siberia and Alaska contains the slowly decomposing remains of ancient vegetation, and as the permafrost thaws this organic matter releases methane, a greenhouse gas roughly thirty times more effective than carbon dioxide at trapping atmospheric heat. The planet's largest refrigerated archive is in the process of converting itself into the planet's largest unregulated emission source. The freezer that has cooled Earth has begun, very slowly, to ignite.
