# Stage 0, Chapter 12: Bodies at Work

This chapter trades the abstract subjects of earlier chapters for living human beings sweating, stooping, and clenching their way through concrete physical labour. Each section pursues a single protagonist through a sequence of bodily actions designed to surface the everyday verbs the rest of the curriculum has had no occasion to use.

---

## 12.1  The Last Blacksmith

Old Zhou is the last man in town who still pounds metal for a living. His son fled the village long ago for an assembly line in a coastal factory, and the soot-blackened shop has been teetering on the edge of closure ever since. One holiday morning the grandson visits, pestering relentlessly for a demonstration, and the old man at last relents and lights the fire. He stoops over the bellows and pumps the wooden handle steadily up and down, until the charcoal flares into a furious orange glow that dazzles anyone foolish enough to stare straight into it. Gripping a long pair of tongs, he buries a lump of dull grey iron deep in the embers and waits patiently while the metal blushes a soft red, then a brighter cherry, then begins to throw off light of its own. He hoists it from the fire and slaps it down onto the anvil. His hammer rises and falls in a steady ringing rhythm, scattering sparks that glitter and die across the floor, beating the lump gradually into the long narrow shape of a blade. Now and then he thrusts the cooling metal back into the embers, softening it again before he can strike further. The final act demands that he hold his breath. He raises the glowing blade and plunges it into a bucket of cold water, where it hisses violently and erupts in a sudden cloud of white steam. This is the quench, and the speed of the plunge determines the hardness of the edge to the fraction of a second. The boy stands transfixed, but the old man only sighs. The craft will die with him. A machine in a coastal factory can stamp out as many blades in a single shift as he can hammer in a season, and no one is willing any longer to spend days bent over an anvil for a single edge.

## 12.2  A Year in the Paddy

The old farmer has rehearsed the same choreography all his life and intends to repeat it once more. When spring at last arrives he soaks a handful of rice seed overnight in a wooden bucket, scatters the seed across the shallow waters of the nursery paddy at dawn, and within days the tender green shoots burst through their husks and reach for the light. He yokes the water buffalo to the wooden plough, the great patient animal panting steadily in the spring heat, and together they drag the iron blade through the compacted earth, turning the soil over furrow after furrow. Once the seedlings have grown tall enough he wades into the wet field and stoops, his back bent at an aching angle for hours on end, transplanting each young shoot one by one into the flooded paddy. He diverts the cool mountain stream into the irrigation channels to flood the rows, and throughout the entire summer he weeds the field beneath a merciless sun, plucking out every rival sprout that competes with the rice for nutrients. By autumn the rice heads have ripened into heavy golden ears, and he swings his curved sickle in long sweeping arcs to reap them, then thrashes the cut bundles against the wooden rim of a tub until the plump grains shed and rain into the bottom. The drudgery of threshing breaks the back of every farmer who has ever lived through it. At last he stores the dried grains in a sealed granary against the coming winter. This entire choreography of spring sowing, summer tending, autumn reaping, and winter storing has been repeated by Chinese farmers across the millennia, slotted precisely into the seasonal nodes of the agricultural calendar. Miss the right week for any single step, and the year's provisions for an entire household will vanish into nothing.

## 12.3  Before Dawn at the Bakery

Long before the first grey light reaches the street the baker has hauled himself out of bed, crossed the dark courtyard, and let himself into the warm shop. He pours flour into a great wooden bowl, sprinkles in salt, dissolves a measure of yeast in tepid water, stirs the yeasted water into the flour with a wide wooden spoon, and pulls everything together with his bare hands. Then he begins to knead, folding the dough onto itself, pressing it down with the heel of his palm, pushing it away across the floured bench, dragging it back and folding it once again, until the sticky lump turns smooth and elastic and bounces gently when poked. He drapes a damp cloth over the bowl and lets the dough rest. Across the hours that follow, invisible yeast cells multiply furiously inside the warm dough, devouring the sugars in the flour and exhaling tiny bubbles of carbon dioxide that swell the lump steadily upward until it has doubled in volume. This is fermentation, and it explains why a risen loaf is soft and airy while an unleavened one bakes as hard as a brick. He punches the dough down again to release the trapped gas, shapes each portion into a smooth round, and slides the loaves into a fiercely hot oven. Inside the oven the sugars and proteins on the surface undergo a brisk chemical reaction called Maillard browning, which conjures both the golden crust and the irresistible aroma that drifts out into the waking street. Bakers, brewers, picklers, and winemakers everywhere have independently domesticated the same invisible microbes: Chinese cooks for soy sauce and rice wine, central Europeans for sourdough and beer, Koreans for kimchi, the whole planet quietly served by the same small living machines.

## 12.4  Ten Seconds on the Track

The sprinter crouches in the starting blocks, every muscle drawn taut as a bowstring at full tension. The starter's pistol has not yet fired and already his heart pounds furiously inside the cage of his ribs, adrenaline floods his bloodstream, his pupils dilate and sharpen every detail along the lane in front of him into eerie clarity. The pistol cracks. He lunges forward, driving off the blocks with explosive force, his arms swinging wildly, his legs hammering down onto the track like pistons in an overheating engine. Within these few violent seconds his body has slipped into a peculiar state. Oxygen cannot possibly arrive at his muscles fast enough, so the muscles improvise an anaerobic shortcut to wring more energy out of their stored sugar, and the byproduct, lactic acid, accumulates rapidly inside the tissue, his veins throbbing along his temples. By the time he crosses the line his legs feel as though molten lead has been poured into them. He stumbles to a halt, hunches over his knees, opens his mouth and gasps for air, his chest heaving up and down, sweat dripping in fat beads off the tip of his nose and onto the rubber of the track. The fascinating biological detail is that human beings are mediocre sprinters by animal standards. A cheetah would have flashed past him as if he were standing still. Yet in stamina humans rank near the very top of the animal kingdom. Our distant ancestors survived precisely by jogging steadily after large prey through the noon heat of the savannah for hours on end, until the prey collapsed of heatstroke and could be finished off at leisure. Persistence, not speed, is what carried our species through.

## 12.5  The Body That Cannot Lie

She sits on a long wooden bench in the hospital corridor waiting for the result of the scan, her fingers wringing the hem of her shirt without her noticing, her brows pulled together into a tight knot of worry. Each time the door at the far end swings open and a nurse steps out, her head jerks upward, her throat clenches, she gulps and swallows hard, her heart leaps into her mouth before sinking again. None of these reactions has she chosen. Under stress the body automatically pumps hormones into the blood, the heart speeds, the palms sweat, the muscles tense, the entire system braces itself in advance for danger. The mechanism was carved into our genes when our ancestors encountered large predators on the savannah and needed, in a single instant, to flee or to fight. At last the doctor pushes open the door and announces it was a false alarm. The tension she has held all morning collapses in a single moment. Her shoulders slump, her body sags into the bench, and tears unbidden well up and spill down her cheeks, surprising even her. People often weep at the abrupt release of intense fear, because once the dam of emotion gives way the body needs an outlet through which to drain. Our faces, more broadly, are constitutionally incapable of concealing what we feel. The dilated pupil, the unconscious micro-expression, the involuntary flinch of the shoulder, the quivering of a lip, all conspire to betray the most practised liar before he has finished his sentence. This is exactly why the reading of faces and the polygraph machine will never go out of business.
