# Stage 0, Chapter 2: Earth History

---

## 2.1  The Great Suffocation

The early Earth had no real surface to speak of. Beneath a roiling atmosphere of steam and corrosive gases, the crust constituted a turbulent ocean of magma that took hundreds of millions of years to cool down sufficiently for solid rock to form. Once the surface had finally hardened, the dense clouds above released a downpour that endured, by some estimates, longer than the entire span of recorded human history, and as the precipitation accumulated in every low basin on the planet, it generated the first true oceans. Deep below those oceans, hydrothermal vents emerged along the volcanic seams in the crust, expelling scalding water saturated with minerals and chemical energy, and within the turbulence surrounding those vents the first organic molecules that could replicate themselves gradually coalesced into something we have agreed to call life. For roughly two billion years afterward, life persisted as a microbial film, consuming dissolved carbon and excreting waste like any thrifty creature. Then a particular cyanobacterium learned to harness sunlight directly, and the by-product of its new metabolism happened to be oxygen. The released oxygen accumulated relentlessly in the seawater and then in the atmosphere, and here arrives the twist nobody ever warns you about: oxygen, the molecule on which your every breath now depends, was profoundly poisonous to the older life that had thrived in its absence. Entire populations of anaerobic microbes were eradicated outright by the exhalations of their distant cousins. This Great Oxygenation Event constituted the first mass extinction the planet had ever endured, and it cleared the slate for every complex creature that would eventually inhabit the world above.

---

## 2.2  The Cambrian Spark

Then the planet froze over. For reasons climatologists still argue about, the atmosphere cooled so drastically that ice advanced from the poles all the way to the equator, and the entire planetary surface, oceans included, was encompassed by a frozen crust kilometres thick. Life persisted, but only in narrow pockets near hydrothermal vents on the seafloor, where heat continued to escape from below. Crucially, the volcanoes themselves never paused: across millions of years they kept generating carbon dioxide into the locked atmosphere, and once enough of that greenhouse gas had accumulated, the ice yielded, sunlight reached the ocean once again, and life received the most spectacular opportunity in its entire history. What we now call the Cambrian Explosion commenced almost simultaneously across every ocean basin on the planet. Animals of forms so bizarre they appear nearly imaginary suddenly emerged in the fossil record: the predatory Anomalocaris with its segmented compound eyes, trilobites armoured in interlocking plates, and a small ribbon-like creature named Pikaia which appeared, at the time, utterly negligible. Yet hidden along Pikaia's slender body lay a thin internal rod called a notochord, and that single anatomical innovation would eventually constitute the backbone of every fish, every reptile, every bird, every mammal, and, with eight hundred million years of patience, you. Here is the strange consolation of being human: trace your own lineage backwards far enough and you arrive at a worm so unremarkable that the giants of its era barely perceived it at all.

---

## 2.3  Out of the Water

For more than two billion years after life first appeared, every living thing remained in the water. The land above was a barren expanse, baked by ultraviolet radiation and devoid of the organic matter that vertebrates would require to survive. Plants ascended onto the shore first, slowly, awkwardly, like reluctant tourists at a hostile resort, but their photosynthesis steadily generated breathable oxygen and gradually transformed the bare rock into the loamy soil we now take for granted. Then a particular lobe-finned fish named Tiktaalik, recovered from the Devonian sediments of arctic Canada, manifested something the world had never seen before: actual wrists, complete with articulated bones that could bend and bear weight. Tiktaalik almost certainly utilised those primitive limbs to crawl through tidal mudflats and ambush prey from the oxygen-rich shallows. Its descendants, with the kind of patience only evolution can demonstrate, traded gills for lungs, refined the wrist into a jointed foot, and eventually constituted the first amphibians and reptiles to inhabit dry ground. Every tetrapod that has ever walked, jumped, flown, or burrowed, from the heaviest elephant down to the smallest field mouse, traces its descent from that single ancestor on the mudflat. The atmosphere of that era was thick with oxygen, and dragonflies, encountering no predator capable of catching them, ascended to dimensions that would terrify a contemporary bird-watcher. Their wingspans reached the size of modern raptors. Imagine swatting at an insect and discovering it is the size of an eagle. That was an ordinary afternoon in the Carboniferous swamp.

---

## 2.4  Pangaea and the Great Dying

Long after life had spread across the continents, the continents themselves drifted slowly together. Two hundred and fifty million years ago every landmass on Earth converged into a single colossal supercontinent that geologists now designate Pangaea, surrounded by a single ocean almost too immense to comprehend. The coastline, suddenly compressed into one continuous loop, dwindled drastically, and the vast interior of Pangaea, far from any sea breeze that might have moderated its climate, deteriorated into burning desert. Then catastrophe commenced. Across what is now Siberia, fissures opened in the crust and persisted for almost a million years, expelling lava across an area larger than modern Europe and discharging volcanic gases into the atmosphere on a scale the planet had never previously encountered. Sulphur dioxide contaminated the air. The oceans absorbed so much carbon dioxide that they turned acidic and the dissolved oxygen they had once contained dwindled to almost nothing, which only exacerbated the suffering of every marine creature struggling to breathe. The result was the Permian extinction, the most ruinous episode of mass dying the planet has ever endured. Roughly ninety-six per cent of every marine species on Earth was eradicated, along with the majority of land vertebrates and the great forests that had thrived for tens of millions of years. Life as a whole came closer to vanishing entirely than at any other moment in its history. The few species that managed to withstand the disaster received a stage that had been almost completely cleared, and from that small surviving remnant the entire next chapter of the planet would have to be improvised.

---

## 2.5  The Rise of Giants

Onto the empty stage stepped the survivors, and they refused to apologise for being alive. One particular line of reptiles, modest in size at the outset, evolved across the next thirty million years into the most successful tetrapods the planet would ever produce. These were the dinosaurs, and they encompassed every conceivable ecological niche so quickly that no rival vertebrate could displace them. Giant predators ascended to the top of the food chain on land. Long-necked sauropods consumed the canopies of entire forests in a single afternoon. Pterosaurs, technically not dinosaurs but their close kin, articulated immense leathery wings and patrolled the skies above. Plesiosaurs occupied the seas, propelled by paddled limbs and capable of seizing fish at depths most other reptiles could not endure. For one hundred and sixty-five million years, a duration roughly four hundred times longer than human civilisation has yet persisted, the dinosaurs simply prevailed. And while they prevailed, an event of equal significance was unfolding beneath their feet. Pangaea had begun to fragment. The supercontinent split, and split again, and the resulting plates drifted apart in slow motion, each one carrying its own community of dinosaurs, its own plants, and its own gradually diverging climate into isolation. That is how each modern continent eventually arrived at such a divergent collection of native animals: not by accident, but through the patient mechanism of plates separating in opposite directions, transporting their cargo of evolving lineages across the surface of the Earth like enormous rafts that no one had ever launched on purpose.

---

## 2.6  The Asteroid

Sixty-six million years ago a stone roughly ten kilometres across, an unannounced fragment of debris orbiting in the inner solar system, accelerated downward through Earth's atmosphere at twenty kilometres per second and collided with the shallow coast of what is now the Yucatán Peninsula. The collision generated an explosion approximately a billion times more energetic than every nuclear weapon humans have ever built combined. The impact ejected a curtain of incinerated rock into the upper atmosphere, where it ascended into orbital trajectories and then rained down across every continent as a global hailstorm of glowing glass. Tsunamis dispersed outward from the impact site, vast walls of water that eroded coastlines on every shore of every ocean. Dust enveloped the world for years afterwards and obscured the Sun so persistently that photosynthesis nearly ceased on Earth's surface. Plants withered. The herbivores starved. The predators that had consumed those herbivores starved as well. The entire complex food chain, maintained throughout the long Mesozoic, collapsed inside what geologists call a single instant. Roughly three quarters of every species on the planet was eradicated, including every dinosaur larger than a chicken. But a few small mammals had been quietly inhabiting underground burrows, hibernating through the worst of the dust winter on stored fat, and their lineage endured. Above them, where the dinosaurs had ruled for one hundred and sixty-five million years, the surface stood almost completely cleared, an open inheritance waiting for whoever would dare to claim it. The mammals were about to dare.

---

## 2.7  How a Tree Made You

Once the giants were gone, the planet belonged to the mammals. Across the next sixty million years they accumulated the survival features we now take entirely for granted: dense fur for thermal regulation, live birth and milk for the careful nurturing of offspring, and a warm-blooded metabolism that allowed them to remain active through climatic conditions that had previously eliminated their competitors. They diverged into whales beneath the surface of every ocean, into bats articulating leathery wings through every night sky, and into moles dispersing patiently through the soil itself. One particularly modest branch of these mammals ascended into the canopies of tropical forests and constituted the first primates. Life among the branches demands precision: to leap from one bough to the next without missing, the brain must perceive depth accurately, and the fingers must grasp with a force calibrated for each delicate landing. So stereoscopic vision and articulated thumbs emerged together over millions of years, an adaptation induced upon the lineage by the geometry of the trees themselves. Then the climate cooled. Forests across Africa diminished, and one particular line of primates was compelled to descend to the open savannah, where, instead of crawling on all fours like every other surviving mammal, they began to walk upright on two legs. The freed pair of forelimbs accumulated stones, manipulated sticks, kindled the first fires, and ignited the long refinement of tools. The brain demanded by all this manipulation expanded in size. Meat consumption rose. Tools multiplied. And from a single dwindling population of African australopithecines, one species eventually emerged that could read sentences like this one and recognise itself in them.
