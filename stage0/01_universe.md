# Stage 0 — Chapter 1: The Universe

---

## 1.1  The First Light

In an epoch so remote that no human concept of time can encompass it, the entirety of the cosmos was condensed into a singularity smaller than an atom, a point of unimaginable density wherein space and time had yet to acquire their familiar properties. From this incomprehensible compression, the universe erupted outward with such violence that it underwent a phase of exponential inflation, expanding at a velocity that defies intuition. Only after aeons of gradual cooling, as the primordial matter slowly dissipated its inconceivable heat, could the first beams of light finally disentangle themselves from the dense fog of particles and propagate freely across the nascent void, bearing witness to the universe's earliest moment of luminosity. That ancient radiance, now stretched and attenuated by the relentless expansion of space, still permeates the cosmos in every direction as a faint microwave background; indeed, a small fraction of the static hiss audible between the stations of an old radio is the lingering whisper of that primordial dawn.

---

## 1.2  The Devouring Dark

When a giant star burns through the last of its fuel, gravity wins. The star buckles and crushes itself into a point so small that the laws of physics give up at the doorstep. Around this point hangs an invisible boundary called the event horizon: cross it, and nothing — not signals, not heat, not even light, the fastest messenger the universe has — can ever climb back out. Stranger still, time itself slows as you approach. To a friend watching from far away, you would never quite fall in; you would slow, redden, and freeze forever at the rim, while in your own frame you plunged on and were stretched into a thread of matter — astronomers call this spaghettification, the only physics term that makes you laugh on the way to your death. These quiet monsters are not rare. One sits at the heart of nearly every great galaxy, including our own Milky Way, swallowing gas, dust, and the occasional unlucky star without sound, without warning, without end.

---

## 1.3  Unseen Currents

The Sun is not special. It is one of perhaps a hundred billion stars in the Milky Way, and the Milky Way itself is one galaxy among trillions, all of them turning together around centres we cannot see. When astronomers measured how fast these galaxies spin, they ran into a problem: the stars on the outer edges should, by every law we know, be flung off into the void. They aren't. Something is holding them in place — something we cannot see, cannot touch, and cannot detect with any instrument we have ever built. We have named this invisible glue dark matter, mostly because we needed something to call it. Then it got worse. The whole universe, it turned out, is not just expanding but speeding up, dragged apart by something even stranger we have named dark energy. Add the numbers up and the conclusion lands like a slap: every star, planet, atom, and person we have ever seen or measured makes up about five per cent of what is actually out there. The other ninety-five per cent remains, to this day, a complete mystery.

---

## 1.4  The Speed That Bends Time

Light, when it travels through empty space, sets the speed limit of the universe. Nothing can outrun it. And the closer you push toward that limit, the more time itself starts to slow down for you. Send one twin off in a fast spacecraft and keep the other at home: when the traveller returns, she will have aged less than her sister, not as a trick of perception but as a fact you could measure with two clocks. Gravity does the same thing. An astronaut who spends a year aboard the Space Station comes home a few thousandths of a second younger than her crewmates on the ground, because she has been orbiting both faster and farther from the pull of the planet below. That sounds like a curiosity until you remember the satellites guiding the map on your phone: they live in slightly slower time than you do, and if the engineers did not correct for that gap every single second, your maps would drift kilometres off course within a day. Einstein predicted all of this a century ago from a desk and a pencil. Every experiment ever designed to prove him wrong has, instead, proved him right.

## 1.5  The Solar Hearth

The sunlight that warms your face this afternoon is older than human civilisation. A ray of light begins its life as a burst of energy deep inside the Sun's core, but the plasma packed around it is so dense that the new-born photon bounces off neighbour after neighbour, scrambling outward almost at random, and the journey from core to surface takes, on average, a hundred thousand years. Once it finally breaks free, the trip across space to your skin takes only eight minutes. The Sun keeps the furnace burning by converting its own substance into pure energy, and somehow this enormous outflow has barely scratched its reserves. But our nearest star is not a friendly hearth. In 1859 the Sun hurled a storm of charged particles directly at Earth. Auroras lit up the night sky as far south as Cuba. Telegraph operators received shocks from machines they had unplugged. Sparks leapt from wires that supposedly ran to nothing. Several offices caught fire. Were a storm of that size to strike today, satellites would die, power grids could collapse for months, and we would learn, abruptly, how thin the cable that hangs our civilisation between us and the furnace really is.

---

## 1.6  A Cradle Among Worlds

Earth, in its early days, was very nearly destroyed. A rogue planet about the size of Mars — astronomers, working backwards from the wreckage, gave it the name Theia — slammed into the molten young Earth at an angle, vaporised the crust, and flung a glowing cloud of rubble into orbit. From that ruin came a gift: the cloud, over time, drew itself together into the Moon. The new Moon hung close enough to fill the sky like a second sun, and its gravity dragged tides of monstrous height across the boiling seas. Days raced past in six hours. The whole planet spun like a top. Then, very slowly, the Moon backed away and the spinning settled, and that same Moon now holds Earth's axis steady at the tilt that gives us seasons — without which the climate would lurch and slide, and life as we know it might never have caught hold. The Moon is not even our only quiet guardian. The magnetic field that turns away the Sun's lethal radiation is no fixed shield: every few hundred thousand years it weakens, sputters, and flips, swapping north and south outright; the compass of any civilisation living through such a flip would spin in confusion, hunting for a north that had quietly walked to the bottom of the world.

---

## 1.7  The Great Silence

Telescopes have by now found thousands of planets orbiting other stars. A handful of them are roughly the size of Earth, sitting at just the right distance from their suns for liquid water to gather on the surface — the same recipe that produced us. Meanwhile, immense radio dishes sweep the sky, listening patiently for any signal that another civilisation has sent its voice out across the dark. Year after year, nothing. The silence is the disturbing part. The universe has existed for so long, and contains so many stars, that the galaxy ought to be loud with neighbours — civilisations far older and cleverer than ours, broadcasting freely, perhaps even visiting. And yet no clear signal, no clear visitor, has ever shown up. The physicist Enrico Fermi summed up the puzzle in five plain words over lunch one day: where is everybody? The answers nobody likes go like this. Maybe civilisations always destroy themselves before they learn to talk to the stars. Maybe they are out there but deliberately hiding from beginners like us. Or — most unsettling of all — maybe we really are alone, the first ones, the only ones, calling out into a silence that has no one to answer.
