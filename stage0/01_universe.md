# Stage 0 — Chapter 1: The Universe

---

## 1.1  The First Light

In an epoch so remote that no human concept of time can encompass it, the entirety of the cosmos was condensed into a singularity smaller than an atom, a point of unimaginable density wherein space and time had yet to acquire their familiar properties. From this incomprehensible compression, the universe erupted outward with such violence that it underwent a phase of exponential inflation, expanding at a velocity that defies intuition. Only after aeons of gradual cooling, as the primordial matter slowly dissipated its inconceivable heat, could the first beams of light finally disentangle themselves from the dense fog of particles and propagate freely across the nascent void, bearing witness to the universe's earliest moment of luminosity. That ancient radiance, now stretched and attenuated by the relentless expansion of space, still permeates the cosmos in every direction as a faint microwave background; indeed, a small fraction of the static hiss audible between the stations of an old radio is the lingering whisper of that primordial dawn.

---

## 1.2  The Devouring Dark

When a star of prodigious mass finally exhausts the nuclear fuel that has long sustained its luminous defiance against gravity, it can no longer withstand the inexorable inward pull of its own colossal weight and undergoes a catastrophic collapse, compressing the entirety of its substance into a region so minute that the laws of conventional physics falter at the threshold. Around this engulfing maw stretches an invisible boundary known as the event horizon, beyond which not even light, the swiftest envoy of the universe, can muster the velocity necessary to escape its grasp. The closer one ventures to such an abyss, the more sluggishly time itself unfolds, so that an unfortunate traveller plunging toward it would, from a distant observer's vantage, appear to decelerate into eternal stillness while being elongated into a slender thread of matter — a grotesque process astronomers have wryly christened spaghettification. Far from being rare aberrations, these silent leviathans are now believed to lurk at the very heart of nearly every great galaxy, including our own Milky Way, quietly siphoning gas, dust, and the radiance of luckless stars into oblivion.

---

## 1.3  Unseen Currents

Our Sun, for all its searing magnificence, is but one undistinguished ember among the hundreds of billions that constitute the Milky Way, all of them wheeling in stately procession around a hidden centre across spans of time that dwarf the totality of recorded human history. Yet astronomers, scrutinising the velocities at which these stars revolve, have long been confronted with an unsettling discrepancy: the galaxies spin too rapidly to be held together by the meagre gravity of the matter we can perceive, betraying the presence of some pervasive, invisible substance whose nature continues to elude every instrument humankind has devised. This enigmatic constituent, provisionally christened dark matter, is now thought to outweigh ordinary matter by roughly a factor of five, scaffolding the cosmos like an invisible web upon which luminous galaxies are merely glittering sequins. To compound the mystery, the entire universe is not merely expanding but accelerating outward, propelled by an even more elusive agent dubbed dark energy, which is steadily dragging every distant galaxy farther beyond our observational reach.

---

## 1.4  The Speed That Bends Time

The propagation of light through a vacuum constitutes the unyielding ceiling of the universe, an absolute limit that no particle, signal, or messenger may transgress, however cunningly it is accelerated. As one approaches this sacrosanct velocity, time itself begins to slacken its tempo: a traveller hurtling outward in a swift spacecraft and returning years later would find that her stationary kin had aged appreciably more than she had, the discrepancy in their respective clocks a stark and measurable verdict of physics rather than a metaphysical curiosity. Gravity, too, has been shown to dilate the passage of time, so that an astronaut residing for a year aboard the International Space Station, perched higher in Earth's gravitational well and orbiting at considerable speed, returns home a trifle younger than her counterparts on the surface — a discrepancy of mere fractions of a second, yet one that the satellite navigation systems guiding our daily commutes must continually compensate for, lest our maps drift into incoherence. Such audacious predictions, first propounded by Einstein in the early decades of the twentieth century, have been corroborated by every meticulous experiment ever conducted in the hope of refuting them.

---

## 1.5  The Solar Hearth

The Sun, an ordinary yellow dwarf of middling stature, ceaselessly transmutes hydrogen into helium within its scorching core, and the prodigious energies thereby liberated radiate outward to illuminate and warm an entire retinue of planets that coalesced from the residual disc of dust and rubble swirling about its infancy. Nearest to the searing crucible orbits Mercury, a scorched and cratered cinder; next comes Venus, perpetually shrouded in suffocating cloud whose pressure would crush an unwary visitor; then our own clement blue Earth; followed by rusty, desiccated Mars; while farther out wheel the colossal gaseous behemoths Jupiter and Saturn, the latter girdled by rings of ice and gravel of unsurpassed splendour; and brooding in the frigid outskirts loiter the remote ice giants Uranus and Neptune, swathed in perpetual twilight. The Sun's mottled visage is periodically blemished by dark sunspots and convulsed by violent flares, eruptions of charged particles potent enough to incapacitate orbiting satellites, disrupt terrestrial communications, and, more benignly, kindle the shimmering auroras that flicker like silken curtains above the polar latitudes.

---

## 1.6  A Cradle Among Worlds

Earth orbits at a felicitous distance from the Sun, sufficiently close to elude perpetual frost yet remote enough to escape incineration, occupying the so-called habitable zone in which water can persist as a liquid upon its surface — the indispensable prerequisite of every form of life we have ever known. In its volcanic infancy our planet was a roiling sphere of molten rock, whereupon the heavier metals, chiefly iron and nickel, gradually sank toward the centre and congealed into a dense core whose churning currents continue to generate the magnetic field that deflects harmful solar radiation and shields the fragile atmosphere above. Volcanoes belched torrents of steam and noxious gases that condensed into the earliest oceans and constituted a primordial atmosphere, while a Mars-sized interloper collided obliquely with the youthful Earth and ejected a glowing cloud of debris that gradually coalesced in orbit into our Moon — a companion whose gravitational tug ceaselessly hauls the seas back and forth in tides, perhaps sculpting the rhythmic surge that coaxed the first tentative organisms from the briny shallows onto the inhospitable shore.

---

## 1.7  The Great Silence

Astronomers, armed with ever more sensitive telescopes, have by now catalogued thousands of planets revolving around distant stars, and a small but tantalising handful among them are roughly Earth-sized, basking in temperate orbits where liquid water might conceivably pool upon their surfaces. Sprawling radio observatories sweep the heavens with patient, methodical scrutiny, eavesdropping for any whisper of an extraterrestrial broadcast, yet decade after decade the celestial airwaves remain stubbornly, almost mockingly mute. This pervasive silence is itself profoundly disquieting: given the staggering antiquity and prodigious vastness of the cosmos, our galaxy ought, by any reasonable reckoning, to be thoroughly populated by civilisations far more ancient and accomplished than our own — and yet not a single unambiguous emissary has ever presented itself, a paradox immortalised in the rueful query of the physicist Enrico Fermi: "Where is everybody?" Perhaps such civilisations habitually obliterate themselves before mastering interstellar communication; perhaps they deliberately cloak their presence from cosmic neophytes like ourselves; or perhaps, more unsettlingly still, we are genuinely alone in the howling dark.
