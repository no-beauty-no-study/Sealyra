# Stage 0, Chapter 1: The Universe

---

## 1.1  The First Light

In an epoch so remote that no human concept of time can encompass it, the entirety of the cosmos was condensed into a singularity smaller than an atom, a point of unimaginable density wherein space and time had yet to acquire their familiar properties. From this incomprehensible compression, the universe erupted outward with such violence that it underwent a phase of exponential inflation, expanding at a velocity that defies intuition. Only after aeons of gradual cooling, as the primordial matter slowly dissipated its inconceivable heat, could the first beams of light finally disentangle themselves from the dense fog of particles and propagate freely across the nascent void, bearing witness to the universe's earliest moment of luminosity. That ancient radiance, now stretched and attenuated by the relentless expansion of space, still permeates the cosmos in every direction as a faint microwave background; indeed, a small fraction of the static hiss audible between the stations of an old radio is the lingering whisper of that primordial dawn.

---

## 1.2  The Devouring Dark

When a giant star burns through the last of its fuel, gravity wins. The star buckles and crushes itself inward until all of its mass is squeezed into a single point so small that the known laws of physics break down at the threshold. Around this point hangs an invisible boundary called the event horizon, and once anything crosses that boundary, nothing returns: not radio signals, not heat, not even light, which is normally the fastest thing in the cosmos. Stranger still, time itself slows the closer you drift toward such a hole. If a spacecraft fell in and a distant observer watched its descent from a safe orbit, she would never see the craft actually reach the centre; she would watch its light dim, its colours redden, and the whole craft hover frozen at the rim of the horizon for the rest of cosmic history, while in its own reference frame the spacecraft plunged on and was stretched into a thin stream of plasma. These black holes are not rare beasts at the edge of the universe. One sits at the heart of almost every galaxy, including our own Milky Way, swallowing gas, dust, and the occasional unlucky star without sound, without warning, without end. The next surprise is even harder to swallow.

---

## 1.3  Unseen Currents

The Sun is not special. It is one of about a hundred billion stars inside the Milky Way, and the Milky Way is one galaxy among trillions, all of them turning together around centres of mass that no telescope has ever directly seen. When astronomers measured the orbital speeds of stars near the edge of a galaxy, they ran into a problem: those outer stars were sweeping around so fast that, by every gravitational law we know, the whole galaxy should have flung itself apart long ago. Yet the galaxies were not flying apart. Something invisible was holding the stars in their orbits, something nobody could see, nobody could weigh, and nobody could detect with any instrument we had ever built. Astronomers gave this invisible mass a name, dark matter, mostly because they needed something to call it. And then it got stranger. The whole cosmos, it turned out, is not merely expanding but accelerating outward, dragged apart by an even more elusive presence now known as dark energy. Add the totals up and the verdict is uncomfortable: every star, every planet, every atom we have ever seen or measured makes up only about five per cent of the universe. The other ninety five per cent stays, for now, a complete blank.

---

## 1.4  The Speed That Bends Time

Light, when it travels through empty space, sets the speed limit of the universe, and nothing else in nature is allowed to overtake it. The closer anything pushes toward that limit, the more time itself begins to slow down for whatever is moving. Imagine a spacecraft that accelerates outward from Earth and cruises for years at close to light speed, then turns around and flies home. The astronaut aboard would step out of her capsule having aged only a little, while the planet she left behind would have moved through far more years of its own, not as a trick of perception but as a fact you could read directly off two clocks held side by side. Gravity does the same thing to time. An astronaut who spends a year on a space station orbiting above the atmosphere returns a few thousandths of a second younger than her crewmates who stayed on the surface, because she has been moving faster and standing farther from the pull of the planet beneath her. That tiny gap sounds like a curiosity until you remember the navigation satellites in orbit overhead: their clocks tick at a slightly different rate from the clocks on the ground, and if engineers did not correct for that gap every single second, the position signals beamed down from those satellites would drift kilometres off course inside a single day. Einstein predicted all of this a century before any of it could be measured, and every experiment ever designed to prove him wrong has, instead, confirmed him.

---

## 1.5  The Solar Hearth

The sunlight that warms your face is older than human civilisation itself. Far down inside the Sun, where the temperature climbs into the millions and hydrogen atoms are crushed together hard enough to fuse into helium, a fresh photon is born in a flash of pure energy. But the plasma packing the Sun's core is so dense that the new photon cannot fly straight outward; instead it bounces off neighbour after neighbour, scattering in every direction, and the journey from core to solar surface takes, on average, about a hundred thousand years. Once that photon finally breaks free of the surface and enters the vacuum of space, the trip across to Earth takes only another eight minutes. The Sun keeps the furnace running by converting its own mass into pure radiation, second by second, and even at that rate the hydrogen it still holds in reserve is enough to last billions of years more. But our nearest star is not gentle. Every now and again the Sun hurls a vast storm of charged particles directly at our planet, and when one of those solar storms slams into Earth's magnetic field, the whole field shudders and rings. On a quiet day the collision merely lights up the polar sky with shimmering aurorae; on a violent day the same storm cripples satellites in orbit, drowns navigation signals in static, and reminds us that the calm yellow disc in our sky is not a hearth at all, but a continuous, controlled explosion held together by its own crushing weight.

---

## 1.6  A Cradle Among Worlds

Earth, in its first hours of existence, was very nearly destroyed. A rogue planet about the size of Mars, which astronomers working backwards from the wreckage have given the name Theia, slammed into the molten young Earth at an angle, vaporised most of the crust, and flung a glowing cloud of rubble into orbit. Out of that ruin came a gift: the cloud, over thousands of years, slowly drew itself together under its own gravity and condensed into the Moon. When the new Moon was young it hung close enough to fill almost half the sky, and its gravity dragged tides of colossal height across the still boiling oceans below. Days passed in only a handful of hours, because the planet was rotating on its axis at a speed it has never reached again. Then, over hundreds of millions of years, the Moon drifted slowly outward and the rotation steadied, and today that same Moon holds Earth's axis fixed at the tilt that gives us our seasons. Without that gentle hand on our axis the climate would lurch and slide across the whole planet every few thousand years, and life as we know it might never have taken hold. The Moon is not even our only quiet guardian. The magnetic field that turns away the lethal radiation streaming off the Sun is no fixed shield: every few hundred thousand years that field weakens, sputters, and flips outright, so that the magnetic north pole quietly migrates down to the southern hemisphere and the south pole climbs up to the north, leaving every compass on the planet pointing the wrong way around for an entire era.

---

## 1.7  The Great Silence

Telescopes have by now identified thousands of planets in orbit around other stars. A handful of them are roughly the size of Earth and sit at just the right distance from their parent stars for liquid water to gather on the surface, which is the same recipe that produced us. Meanwhile, immense radio dishes sweep the sky continuously, listening for any signal that another civilisation might have aimed its voice out across the dark. Year after year, decade after decade, nothing comes back. The silence itself is what nags. The universe has existed for so long, and contains so many stars and so many planets, that the galaxy ought to be crowded with neighbours, civilisations far older and cleverer than ours, broadcasting freely, perhaps even crossing interstellar space to visit. And yet not one signal, not one craft, not one unambiguous trace of another mind has ever appeared in any telescope. The physicist Enrico Fermi summed up the puzzle in five plain words: where is everybody? The answers nobody likes go like this. Perhaps every civilisation destroys itself before it ever learns to talk between the stars. Perhaps the older civilisations out there are deliberately staying quiet around beginners like us. Or, most unsettling of all, perhaps we really are alone in the cosmos, the first ones to emerge, the only ones broadcasting, calling out into a silence that has no one inside it to answer back.
